Plan-1 (frozen 2026-11-27)

1. Goal

Teams write tests as plain-language Gherkin scenarios. There are two ways to run them:

  • Step definitions. Code a developer writes for each test line and keeps up to date after every interface change.
  • Browser agents. AI models that read the scenario and operate the browser themselves, with no code to update.

The study asks whether lower-tier AI agents are good enough to run these tests, compared with step definitions kept up to date, and what they cost. It also measures the gap right after an interface change, when the old step definitions no longer work.

Main results, fixed before any run. For each agent and app, the unit is a scenario with its bug on one version (payment app) or at one change (Moodle):

  • Catch rate. Of all bugs confirmed by the independent check, the share the agent reliably catches. Reliably means caught in at least 2 of 3 runs with the bug, while in at least 2 of its 3 runs without the bug the same step passed.
  • Catch retention. The same, counted only over the bugs the up-to-date step definitions reliably catch.
  • False alarms. The share of runs without a bug that failed, and the chance that a whole suite run on a correct build is all green.

Headline results are catch retention and false alarms, each with a 95% Wilson interval with the scenario as the unit (Wilson intervals stay valid at 0% and 100%), and the per-scenario counts out of 3 behind them. Catch rate, the all-green suite chance and best-of-3 use are reported as one column or one sentence each.

Good enough. An agent is good enough on an app when its catch retention reaches a set level and its false alarms stay below a set level. The two levels come from published figures (pre-task P0b, done):

  • Catch retention: at least 95%. Facebook's predictive test selection reports catching over 95% of test failures, and calls that guarantee sufficient (Machalica et al., ICSE-SEIP 2019).
  • False alarms: at most 1.5% of runs without a bug. About 1.5% of Google's test runs report a flaky result (Micco, Google Testing Blog, 2016). No source states an acceptable rate; this is the documented rate a large maintained suite lives with.

These are strict for agents. With the planned sample sizes, a "good enough" verdict needs a near-perfect result, so the range of levels shown next to the verdict is where most readers will look.

The verdict is good enough, not good enough or inconclusive (the interval crosses the level). The paper also shows how the verdict changes across a range of levels, so a reader can apply their own.

Cells without a valid result (section 9) are left out and reported. Moodle results are estimates; Moodle gets a verdict only if it reaches 20 scenarios.

The gap after a change. For each changed version or Moodle change: the share of scenarios the step definitions from before the update fail without a bug, and the agents' catch rate and false alarms on the same scenarios.

3. Apps, changes and bugs

Payment app

  • Original (v0): the app as published.
  • Change 1 (v1) and change 2 (v2, built on change 1) are specified by a separate blind session (pre-task P4c). It works only from the published web-test breakage types (WATERFALL and the studies it summarises) and the app's code. It never sees the scenarios' step definitions, the agents, the prompt or any run. Its specs are frozen before the prompt is tuned and before the step definitions are updated.

The changes keep the app's behavior the same, so a correct test passes on every version without a bug. The agents see the same scenario text on every version.

Hold-out scenarios. The first 12 scenarios were used in earlier exploratory runs that shaped this design. Eight new scenarios are written by a session that never saw those runs, the agents or the prompt (pre-task P4d). Results are reported for all 20 and for the 8 hold-out scenarios alone. A short log lists what the exploratory runs changed in the design.

Moodle

Each change is a real Moodle commit in which the developers changed the interface and fixed their own step definitions. Pre-task P7c applied the rule below to all 21 mined changes; 5 are eligible, with 19 scenario units; one (B05/S044) was then excluded, leaving 18 units over 15 distinct scenarios (S027 to S029 are units under both B01 and B07, run on different commits):

  • B01 (MDL-85795) encloses the navigation-bar search widget in a group and relabels its button. Scenarios S027, S028, S029.
  • B04 (MDL-87662) moves the manual completion button into the activity header. Scenarios S036, S037, S040, S041.
  • B05 (MDL-81608) replaces the quiz page-break buttons' titles with labels and new strings. Scenarios S042, S043, S045, S046 and S047 (S044 excluded: at about 4 tool calls per user step, as measured in the smoke check, its 136 steps need about 544 calls, above the 250-call limit; rule fixed before any run).
  • B07 (MDL-87834) redesigns the inline search box in the navigation bar. Scenarios S027, S028, S029.
  • B15 (MDL-85281) restyles the "Add block" buttons. Scenarios S091, S093, S094.

The other 16 were rejected with recorded reasons: 13 do not build on the current runtime, and 3 have no scenario both approaches can run. 18 units is below the target of 20, so Moodle gives estimates only, without a verdict.

Rule for adding changes, fixed before any run. Take all 21 mined changes that broke at least one scenario, in history order. The mining query and its output are published. A change is eligible if both hold:

  • It builds: the commits just before and just after it build into a working Moodle site on the current runtime (PHP 8.3, Postgres 17, pinned images), and its scenarios' test setup runs.
  • Both approaches can run it: at least one touched scenario passes with the developers' step definitions on the commit before the change, and the setup can prepare its data so the agent receives only the steps a user performs.

Steps no user can see. Some Moodle developer steps name things only code can see, such as a CSS class or a field id. The coded run keeps the original text. The agent gets a plain-language rewording of only those steps, with the same number of steps and the same targeted step, written before any run and published with the scenarios. The paper says how many steps were reworded (rule added 27 Nov after the scenario review).

Scenarios run with their text at each commit. Where the developers also edited a scenario's text in the change, the agents additionally run the old text on the commit after the change: no one updates an agent's scenarios either. B04, B07 and B15 go through the same rule as the rest. The 2021 changes do not meet it because they do not build on the current runtime. The rule uses no outcome of any agent. All eligible changes are included, and the rejected ones are listed with their reason. The target is at least 20 Moodle scenarios; if the eligible changes give fewer, the paper says so plainly.

Bugs

Every bug re-creates a real, published failure. For each scenario, pre-task P7b applied a frozen rule:

  1. Prefer a real fix from the app's history that can be reverted at the tested commit and breaks the behavior the scenario describes.
  2. Otherwise, re-create a specific real bug report (Moodle tracker or the app's GitHub issues) in the same kind of component, cited by its ID.

Amendment, 27 Nov, before any bug was chosen. The payment app is a small demo, and its history (139 commits, 188 issues) has no real bug that fits the scenarios. For the payment app, step 2 may therefore re-create a specific real bug report from another open-source web application's public tracker, with the same failure in the same kind of component (for example a save that does nothing, a wrong amount saved, or a link to the wrong page). It is cited by its ID and labelled re-created from a real report. Moodle bugs come from Moodle's own history and tracker first. Second amendment, 27 Nov, before those bugs were chosen: for the 8 Moodle units where Moodle's own tracker had no fitting report (the six search-banner units and two block units), the same rule applies as for the payment app, with a report from another well-known open-source web application's tracker. The other 11 Moodle bugs come from Moodle's tracker.

The rule never looks at either approach. Each bug is confirmed by an independent check that reads the app's state or page directly, not through the step definitions or an agent. The check confirms the bug really happens and is visible at the scenario's targeted step. Bugs are written with a short symptom description before any run.

Every bug is code in the harness. Each bug is committed as its own folder, bugs/<bug-id>/, before any run:

  • patch.diff: the code change that puts the bug into the app.
  • source.md: the real fix commit it reverts, or the real bug report it re-creates, with link and ID.
  • symptom.md: what goes wrong and at which scenario step it shows.
  • check.mjs: the independent check that confirms the bug, without the step definitions or an agent.
  • applies-to.json: the app versions the patch applies to.

The prebuilt images are built from these folders, and the bug's ID and source appear in each run's record and in the run grid.

What the rule produced. No revertible real fix fitted any scenario. All 38 bugs are therefore re-created from a real bug report: 10 from Moodle's own tracker and 28 from other open-source projects' trackers. Nine reports serve two or more bugs. The paper calls them bugs re-created from real bug reports, never "real bugs", and reports where each came from. Each bug is built so its failure shows at the step the scenario checks; whether a test catches it is what the study measures.

4. The approaches

  • Step definitions, up to date (payment app). Made once, before any run (pre-task P5): Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code updates them after each change until every scenario passes on the version without bugs. Locators may change and actions the new interface requires may be added; no check is removed or weakened, and every change the diff check flags is reviewed and recorded. Each result is committed in the harness, and runs check it out. Each update is done in 3 separate sessions; the first session that passes provides the code for the runs, fixed before the others are looked at. Claude Code's records give each session's tokens, minutes and dollars, so the spread of maintenance cost is visible. Opus is not one of the agents, so no model both maintains the tests and runs them.
  • Step definitions, before the update (payment app). The previous version's step definitions run on the changed version.
  • Developers' step definitions, before and after the change (Moodle). The before code comes from the commit before the change; the after code is the developers' own fix.
  • Agents. All in Codex CLI 0.169.0, with Playwright MCP 0.0.84 as the only tool, network limited to the app, and a limit of 15 minutes and 250 tool calls per run:
    • GPT-6 Luna, lower tier, medium effort;
    • GPT-5.6 Terra, lower tier, medium effort;
    • GPT-6.2 Sol, frontier reference, high effort.

Why these models. Running a test suite on every change is a high-volume workload. A team, or a cloud testing service, would pick models that are cheap and fast per run, so the study tests whether lower-tier models are good enough. Sol is the frontier reference: it shows how much a stronger model closes the gap, and at what cost. The top models (GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6 Astra) were not used because of their high cost per run. Running every agent in the same tool means model differences are not mixed up with tool differences.

Prompt. One shared template for both apps: the scenario, the app's address, a short glossary of app terms and the required answer format (pass or fail, the failing step, the reason). The template and glossary are tuned only on practice scenarios that are never scored, then frozen with a hash. The agent sees only the browser, never the bugs, the step definitions or earlier runs.

5. One run

A run is one plan item: one app version, one scenario, with or without its bug, one approach, one repetition. Every item has a fixed ID, for example pay.v1.bank-delete.bug.luna.r2.

  1. Start fresh containers from the prebuilt image for this app variant: version, bug or no bug, database already seeded.
  2. Run the scenario with the approach.
  3. Record the result: pass or fail, the failing step, the reason or assertion message, the full trace, tokens, minutes, image digests and tool versions.
  4. Append the record to the ledger and discard the containers.

Running an item again adds an attempt and keeps the old ones. Only an invalid attempt (the environment failed before the test ran) is rerun. Every other attempt counts as it is, and no run is repeated to get a different answer.

Scoring rules, frozen before any run

  • Caught is mechanical and the same for both approaches. A run with the bug is caught when the failing step it reports is the targeted step. For step definitions, the failing step comes from the test runner. For agents, it comes from the step named in the required answer, matched by its number in the scenario or its exact text. No judge is involved.
  • Reason match is secondary and stays in the replication package, not in the paper. Whether the stated reason describes the bug's written symptom is coded by three coders: two Claude Opus sessions and one Codex session. They see the reason text with the approach's identity removed and agent prose reduced to the same short form as assertion messages, so they cannot tell which approach wrote it. Agreement (kappa) is reported. The coders never change any primary result.
  • Worked examples. The scoring code ships with worked examples of every outcome, run as tests before the freeze.

What the paper reports

  • Unit. The scenario with its bug. Per scenario and approach, the paper gives how many of the 3 runs with the bug were caught, and how many of the 3 runs without it were false alarms.
  • Main results. Catch rate, catch retention, false alarms and the verdict from section 1, per agent and app, for one run and for best of 3.
  • Secondary results. Detected at any step, reason match, unresolved runs by cause, results by kind of bug, and Sol as the frontier reference.
  • No significance tests. The study is a descriptive benchmark: counts and Wilson intervals. Most accepted MIPRO SSE empirical papers report results this way (a planning note; the paper does not say this).
  • Cost. One table for every approach: tokens and minutes per suite and per caught bug. For the step definitions it adds the cost of each update from Claude Code's records (tokens, minutes, dollars), the size of each change in code (broken step definitions, changed lines), and a break-even line: how many agent suite runs cost as much as one update. That is set against how often Moodle's interface actually changed in its history, assuming one suite run per commit, so a team can read it as runs per interface change.

A note on the step definitions. Up-to-date step definitions pass every scenario without a bug by the maintenance rule, so their false-alarm count is zero by definition. The paper states this and does not present it as a measured result. Their catches of real bugs are measured: real bugs, chosen independently of them, can slip past their checks.

7. Why 3 repetitions, not 10

Reviewers asked whether 10 runs per cell would be stronger. It would not change the conclusions, for three reasons:

  • The limit is scenarios, not runs. The claims are about scenarios and their bugs, and there are 20 in the payment app plus the Moodle scenarios. More runs of the same scenario make each scenario's estimate a little sharper, but they add no new scenario, bug or interface change. The number of independent units, and with it the strength of every comparison, stays the same.
  • 3 runs already answer the practical question. A team needs to know whether an agent gives the same answer each time. Three runs show that directly: 3 of 3 is consistent, 2 of 3 is shaky, and 1 of 3 is unreliable.
  • 10 runs would more than triple the cost and time of the agent runs, effort better spent on more real Moodle changes, which add independent units.

Comparable studies differ: Bergsmann et al. ran 10 runs, Xu et al. 4, PinATA 1, and none of the accepted MIPRO SSE empirical papers repeats runs.

8. How runs execute

  • Prebuilt container variants. Every app variant is built once before any run, as its own image pinned by digest: each version, with no bug or with each bug, database already seeded. A run only starts images, so it is fast and starts from exactly the same state every time.
  • Isolated and parallel. Every run gets fresh containers. Several workers run at once; the smoke test sets their number. This machine has 20 CPUs and 23 GB.
  • Randomized order. Runs go in time blocks. Within each block, the order of approaches and of runs with and without the bug is shuffled with a recorded seed, so a change in a hosted model during the day affects every approach equally. Provider responses and worker load are logged.
  • Credentials. The Codex login is passed into each container at start, read-only, and never written to logs, traces or the ledger.
  • No slow runs. A hard limit of 5 minutes per run. There is no token cap; tokens are recorded per run.

9. Run control and alerts

The runner stops starting new runs and lets running ones finish when any of these happens:

  • a provider refuses a call: out of credits, quota or rate limit, expired login, or an authentication error;
  • two invalid runs in a row on the same worker or approach;
  • runs of one approach take more than twice their smoke-test time, three times in a row;
  • an all-pass gate or a bug check fails;
  • a worker runs low on memory or disk.

It then sends a Telegram message to the owner (TELEGRAM_CHAT_ID in .envrc) naming the run, the error and what is needed. Runs resume only after the fix.

Missing data, as code

  • An invalid attempt is quarantined and rerun as a new attempt, up to 3 times. After that, the run is reported as missing.
  • Runs stopped by an alert are rerun after the fix; they are never dropped.
  • A cell counts toward the primary claim only when all its runs have a valid result; otherwise it is reported as incomplete.
  • These rules are code in the runner, with tests, before the freeze.

10. Results are kept immutably

  • Append-only ledger. One record per attempt, never edited or deleted. Each record holds the SHA-256 of its trace and of the record before it, so any change to the history is detectable.
  • Raw files. Traces, agent answers and test-runner logs are kept per attempt.
  • Dashboard. Synced from the ledger every 15 minutes and at the end of each batch. The ledger is the record; the dashboard is a view.
  • Snapshots. After every batch, the ledger and raw files are committed to the replication repository with checksums. A summary with the commit and ledger hash is saved in Foundry.
  • Counts. One script computes every paper number from the ledger of the frozen plan and names the ledger hash it read.
  • Published before the runs. The frozen plan, its run-cell list, the scoring code and the bug folders are published as a public, timestamped release of the replication repository before the first measured run. The paper cites it, and states in one sentence that earlier exploratory runs exist and are in the package.

12. What goes into the paper

  • One question, two experiments (payment app, Moodle real changes), and the good-enough claim.
  • Result words: caught, detected elsewhere, false alarm, unresolved. Cost per suite and per caught bug, the cost of each step-definition update, and the break-even line.
  • A short worked example and one study-flow figure.
  • At most 4,000 words and 8 to 9 headings, near the median of accepted MIPRO SSE papers.
  • Related work and novelty. Automated repair of broken web tests (WATER, Vista, LLM-based repair such as Xu et al.) is covered as the alternative to agents. Kim (2026) ran hand-written Playwright scripts and a GPT-4o agent on the same 12 scenarios under a UI change, measuring pass rates only. This study adds:
    • real bugs, run with and without the bug;
    • Gherkin step definitions before and after their update;
    • real Moodle interface changes.
    No "first" claim beyond that combination.
  • Threats.
    • Both apps, their public tests, the Moodle commits and public bug reports may be in the models' training data. Commit dates are listed next to model knowledge cutoffs.
    • Unclear steps can cause false failures.
    • Agents may claim actions they did not take; the traces show the actions.
    • A model updating code can weaken assertions; the diff check prevents this.
    • The bugs re-create published failures but are built by us, and each is built to show at the step its scenario checks. A test that checks that step is therefore well placed to catch it; tests that check other things are not.
    • In Moodle, the developers' step definitions run in a browser without JavaScript, as Moodle's Behat setup does, while the agents' browser runs JavaScript.
    • Interface breakage has causes beyond locators.
    • There are few apps and scenarios.
  • The hold-out scenarios, the Moodle old-text runs and the run infrastructure get one sentence each.
  • Every number comes from the counts script. All data, traces and scripts go to the replication package.
